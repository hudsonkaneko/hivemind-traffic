# How the waypoint simulation works

Documented: 2026-09-11. Sources: [environment](../scripts/waypoint_env.py),
[runner](../scripts/train_waypoint.py), and [parameters](parameters.md).

## The task

One car starts on flat ground. A goal is sampled 2–5 m away, within ±0.65 radians
(about ±37 degrees) of its initial forward direction. The car's initial heading
itself is random over a full circle. It has 20 simulated seconds to get within
0.4 m of the goal. It does **not** have to stop at the goal to succeed.

Success, failure, or timeout resets the car and creates a new target. This is
episodic point-to-point driving, not yet continuous waypoint following. There
are no obstacles, lanes, other traffic, V2V messages, or independent AV policies.

## Software and data flow

```text
OpenUSD Leatherback + ground
          ↓
Isaac Sim / PhysX simulates wheel contact and articulated motion
          ↓
Isaac Lab reads simulator state and forms 9 observation values
          ↓
PPO policy chooses 2 action values
          ↓
Clipping + Ackermann conversion → steering and wheel-drive targets
          ↓
Four physics steps → new state, reward, termination check
```

OpenUSD stores the scene. PhysX computes motion. Isaac Lab supplies the vectorized
reinforcement-learning environment. Stable-Baselines3 implements PPO. Training
uses 16 separated copies of this same one-car task and **one shared policy**.
Those copies collect experience; they are not cooperating vehicles.

Physics runs on the GPU (`cuda:0`). The small PPO neural network is currently
trained and evaluated on the CPU, with four PyTorch CPU threads. CUDA-enabled
PyTorch is installed, but that does not mean the policy optimizer uses the GPU.

## What the policy sees

The 9 values, in order, are:

| Indices | Observation | Scaling |
|---|---|---|
| 0–1 | Goal x/y relative to the car, rotated into its body frame | Divide by 5 m |
| 2–3 | Forward and sideways body-frame velocities | Divide by maximum speed (1 m/s) |
| 4 | Body yaw rate | Raw rad/s |
| 5–6 | Actual left/right front steering joint angles | Divide by 0.5 rad |
| 7–8 | Most recently applied clipped policy commands | Already in [−1, 1] |

These are ground-truth simulator measurements. There is no image recognition,
lidar perception, GPS noise model, or learned world model in this version.

## How actions move the car

The environment clips the two policy outputs to [−1, 1]. Desired speed is
`(action[0] + 1) / 2` m/s, so −1 requests zero speed and +1 requests 1 m/s.
Desired center steering angle is `action[1] * 0.5` radians. Reverse is unavailable.

The controller computes separate inner/outer steering angles and wheel speeds
from the 0.32 m wheelbase, 0.2416 m track, and 0.052 m tire radius. These are drive
targets, not direct changes to world position. The car moves through contact
physics. Root poses and joint states are written directly only during reset.

## Reward and endings

At each 1/30-second control step:

```text
reward = 4 × (previous planar distance − new planar distance)
         − 0.01
         − 0.01 × sum((current clipped action − previous clipped action)²)
         + 20 × reached_target
         − 20 × failed
```

Moving toward the goal earns reward; moving away loses it. The small constant
penalty encourages finishing, and the action-change penalty discourages abrupt
command changes. It does not explicitly minimize energy, jerk, or steering rate.

Failure means the body-up vertical component falls below 0.5 (roughly more than
60 degrees of tilt), local z drops below −1 m or rises above 3 m, or local x/y
exceeds ±48 m. These are geometric checks. **There is no collision-count detector
or near-collision reward in this task.** A timeout occurs at the episode limit.

## How PPO training works

PPO collects trajectories with a stochastic policy, estimates which actions did
better than expected, and updates the policy using a clipped objective to limit
the size of each update. A value network learns to predict future return and
helps estimate these advantages.

The MLP has two 128-unit hidden layers per policy/value branch. No pretrained
driving weights, demonstrations, SUMO simulation, or external dataset were used.
The dataset is experience generated by the simulator. The runner gathers 256
control steps from each of 16 environments (4,096 transitions) before each rollout
update, then performs 5 epochs with minibatches of 256. See the exact values in
[parameters](parameters.md).

Training samples actions to explore. Evaluation uses `predict(...,
deterministic=True)`. The environment still clips those outputs. New trace files
distinguish raw policy actions from applied clipped actions; early trace files
used an `action` field for the raw output.

## How results are measured

The saved summaries count completed episodes as successes, failures, or timeouts.
Success percentage is successes divided by that completed total. In-flight
episodes when a fixed evaluation step budget ends are not included. A successful
policy can complete many more episodes than a slow one in the same step budget.

The initial comparisons used seed 43 for evaluation and seed 42 for training.
They used the same target distribution and step budget, not an explicitly
pre-generated matched list of target scenarios. Different reset timing changes
which random targets each controller receives. The 187/187 result therefore
describes one limited functional check, not universal driving ability or a
statistically established improvement on real traffic.

## Saving and resuming

The SB3 `.zip` file contains policy/value weights, optimizer state, and model
configuration. It does not contain the full physical scene or an exact suspended
simulator episode. Resume loads those learned parameters and starts fresh
environments. The current `learn()` call resets the model's timestep counter for
each invocation; checkpoint filenames alone do not reveal lifetime training.

Output directories and checkpoint names may be reused. The new documentation
recorder preserves a uniquely named manifest and source snapshot for each run,
including input/output checkpoint hashes. Hashes identify weights; they are not
backups of the weights themselves. Use unique `--output` directories to preserve
all artifacts from future experiments.
